Discussion Proposal

A Case for Receive-Only Radio Exemption for Airborne Pilots

Exploring whether the BHPA should advocate for a regulatory change to permit passive ATC monitoring by pilots operating in Class G airspace without an FRTOL.

Prepared: April 2026 For consideration by: BHPA Representatives Status: Discussion Draft

Executive Summary

Under the Wireless Telegraphy Act 2006 (Section 48), it is currently illegal for a pilot without a Flight Radiotelephony Operator's Licence (FRTOL) to monitor ATC frequencies — even passively, even while airborne in shared airspace. This document argues that there is a strong and compelling case for the BHPA to seek a receive-only exemption for airborne pilots, on grounds of safety, proportionality, regulatory consistency, and the protection of life.

The Current Legal Position

Section 48(1) of the Wireless Telegraphy Act 2006 makes it an offence to use wireless telegraphy apparatus with intent to obtain information about the contents, sender, or addressee of any message that the person is not authorised to receive.

The FRTOL provides that authorisation. Without one, a pilot monitoring local ATC frequencies — even in receive-only mode, with no transmission capability and no possibility of interfering with the service — is technically committing a criminal offence carrying a penalty of up to two years' imprisonment.

This means a paramotor pilot flying legally in Class G airspace, where radio carriage is not required, faces a stark choice: fly without any radio awareness of surrounding traffic, or break the law by listening.

The Paradox

A pilot who chooses to be more safe by passively monitoring nearby traffic is, under current law, committing a more serious act than a pilot who flies in complete ignorance of the traffic around them. This cannot be the intent of a framework designed to protect aviation safety.

The Case for Change

There are four strong, complementary arguments for seeking a receive-only exemption.

1

Safety & Collision Avoidance

Passive monitoring of local frequencies gives pilots direct awareness of nearby traffic — their altitude, intentions, and position. In Class G airspace with no mandatory separation service, this awareness directly reduces the risk of mid-air collision.

2

Proportionality

Section 48 was designed to combat espionage, criminal eavesdropping, and commercial piracy. Applying it to a pilot passively monitoring for self-preservation — causing zero interference — is a disproportionate use of the statute.

3

Right to Life (ECHR Art. 2)

Criminalising a pilot's ability to monitor traffic in shared airspace — where the information could prevent a fatal collision — engages fundamental human rights protections under Article 2 of the European Convention on Human Rights.

4

Regulatory Consistency

The CAA actively promotes electronic conspicuity and situational awareness. ADS-B receivers, PilotAware, and FLARM are all encouraged — yet a simple airband receiver providing the same awareness is criminalised.

Safety: The Core of the Argument

The UK Airprox Board regularly analyses near-miss incidents in which lack of situational awareness is identified as a contributing factor. Class G airspace — where paramotors, hang gliders, microlights, GA aircraft, military traffic, and helicopters all share the sky — relies heavily on the principle of see and avoid.

But "see and avoid" has well-documented limitations:

  • Closing speeds between aircraft can exceed 150 knots, leaving seconds to react
  • Small aircraft (including paramotors) are extremely difficult to see visually
  • Weather, sun glare, and cockpit workload further reduce visual detection
  • A radio provides advance warning that no visual scan can match
A Radio Saves Lives

A pilot who hears "Golf-Alpha-Bravo, downwind runway two-four" knows there is traffic in the circuit nearby — information that could take critical seconds or minutes to obtain visually, if it is obtained at all. Even without full radio training, the awareness that traffic is nearby is itself a powerful safety input.

International Comparison

The UK's position is out of step with several comparable aviation nations.

Country Receive-Only Legal? Notes
United Kingdom No Wireless Telegraphy Act 2006, s.48 — no exemption for airborne pilots
United States Yes Fully legal; ATC is treated as public infrastructure. LiveATC streams globally
Canada Yes Receive-only is legal under the Radiocommunication Act
Germany Limited Restricted, but enforcement is minimal and safety use is tolerated
Australia No Similar restrictions to the UK under the Radiocommunications Act 1992

The US and Canadian approach — where ATC is treated as open, transparent public infrastructure — has produced no documented negative safety consequences from receive-only listeners. If anything, the culture of open access has enhanced safety awareness across the broader aviation community.

The Regulatory Inconsistency

The CAA's own initiatives create a striking contradiction with the current legal position:

Technology Function Legal Status
ADS-B IN receiver Passively receives aircraft position broadcasts Encouraged
PilotAware Receives and displays nearby traffic Encouraged
FLARM Receive-only collision avoidance Encouraged
SkyDemon / traffic apps Aggregate traffic data for awareness Encouraged
Airband radio (receive-only) Passively receives local traffic information Criminal offence

All five technologies serve the same fundamental purpose: giving a pilot awareness of nearby traffic to avoid collisions. Yet only one is criminalised. This inconsistency undermines the coherence of the UK's aviation safety framework and deserves serious examination.

Addressing Likely Concerns

1. "Pilots need training to interpret radio communications"

This is a valid concern — and the strongest argument for the FRTOL requirement. However, the exemption sought is not to participate in radio communications but to passively receive them. Even without full phraseology training, a pilot who hears any traffic call on a local frequency gains the critical awareness that other aircraft are operating nearby. This baseline awareness — even without understanding every word — is a net safety gain.

Furthermore, BHPA and training providers could incorporate basic radio awareness into existing pilot training syllabi, ensuring members understand common calls without requiring the full FRTOL.

2. "The FRTOL is easy to obtain — why not just get it?"

The FRTOL is indeed accessible, and we continue to encourage all pilots to obtain one. However, the question is not whether the FRTOL is burdensome — it is whether the law should criminalise a safety-enhancing action by those who have not yet completed it. A new pilot on their first solo flights, still working toward their FRTOL, should not face criminal liability for carrying a receiver that could save their life.

3. "This could be abused by non-pilots"

The proposed exemption would be narrowly scoped to airborne pilots operating in UK airspace, not to the general public. Ground-based scanning by hobbyists would remain unchanged under Section 48. The exemption could be tied to demonstrable pilot status (e.g., BHPA membership, a pilot rating, or an aircraft registration).

What We Are Asking the BHPA to Consider

This proposal does not ask the BHPA to encourage members to break the law. It asks the BHPA to consider whether advocating for a targeted regulatory change would be in the interests of member safety. Specifically:

  1. Raise the issue formally with the CAA, highlighting the safety case and the regulatory inconsistency with the electronic conspicuity agenda.
  2. Propose a narrowly-scoped exemption under the Wireless Telegraphy Act for receive-only use of aviation frequencies by airborne pilots, potentially tied to membership of a recognised aviation body.
  3. Collaborate with allied organisations — the BMAA (British Microlight Aircraft Association), BGA (British Gliding Association), and LAA (Light Aircraft Association) — who share the same interest in Class G situational awareness.
  4. Engage with Ofcom to explore whether a class licence or general authorisation could be created for airborne receive-only use.

Conclusion

The current legal framework criminalises a paramotor pilot for taking a simple, zero-risk, non-interfering action that could prevent a fatal mid-air collision. This is a position that serves neither the interests of aviation safety nor the coherence of UK airspace regulation.

The safety case is clear. The proportionality argument is strong. The regulatory inconsistency is self-evident. And the international precedent — from nations with exemplary aviation safety records — demonstrates that receive-only access to ATC frequencies poses no threat to the integrity of the service.

We respectfully ask the BHPA to consider whether this is an issue worth championing — not just for paramotor pilots, but for every pilot operating in Class G airspace whose safety would be enhanced by the simple act of listening.

"The purpose of aviation regulation is to make flying safer. When a regulation makes flying less safe, it is not serving its purpose — and it is our responsibility to say so." — A principle worth advocating
